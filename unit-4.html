Weeks 7–8

Unit 4: Algorithms, Communication, and Intelligent Transportation

Create and test a transparent traffic-signal decision algorithm.

Duration
Four hours across two weeks
Materials
Paper and pencil; browser optional
Evidence
A tested flowchart or pseudocode table with a written safety limitation.
Unit sequenceObjectives1 Learn2 Experience3 Adapt4 Apply5 ReflectKnowledge check

Learning objectives

  1. Distinguish inputs, conditions, decisions, and outputs in a transportation algorithm.
  2. Trace an algorithm through multiple traffic scenarios and identify unsafe assumptions.
  3. Explain how connected signals can use data while still requiring human oversight.
  4. Adapt a decision-making investigation for a middle-school subject.

Step 1 · Learn

Understand the concept

A traffic controller may use pedestrian requests and vehicle detection to select a safe signal phase. Real intersections require additional safeguards, timing rules, and professional engineering; the model here is a simplified classroom exercise.

Guiding question: Create and test a transparent traffic-signal decision algorithm.

Estimated learning time: 35 minutes. Read the explanation, create a brief concept sketch, and identify one transportation example in your community.

Step 2 · Experience

Model investigation: Decision rules and connected intersections

Allow 60–75 minutes. Work with a partner where possible. Record your reasoning, not just the final answer.

  1. Sketch a four-box input → rule → decision → action diagram for a pedestrian crossing.
  2. Choose an input scenario: pedestrian button pressed or not pressed; traffic detected or not detected.
  3. Apply this classroom rule: if a pedestrian request exists, schedule a WALK phase after vehicles have stopped; otherwise continue the vehicle phase.
  4. Test at least four combinations and record the expected action.
  5. Identify two missing real-world protections (for example minimum clearance time or accessible audible signals).
  6. Revise the rule and explain why your improved algorithm is safer.

Evidence to keep: A tested flowchart or pseudocode table with a written safety limitation.

Step 3 · Adapt

Across subjects and grades

GradeSubjectExample

Technology and unplugged pathways

With technology: Use a shared document, spreadsheet, digital diagram, or classroom presentation to collect and explain evidence. Without technology: Use printed scenario cards, graph paper, drawings, and group discussion to demonstrate the same objective.

Accessibility: Provide written and spoken directions, readable diagrams with text labels, flexible response formats, and group roles. Do not rely only on color or on personal student disclosures.

Standards connection

MS-ETS1-2; MS-ETS1-3 (engineering design connections; not full alignment without performance evidence). These are suggested connections; check the exact performance expectation and your assessment evidence before claiming full standards alignment.

Step 4 · Apply

Classroom transfer plan

  1. Identify the target grade and subject, and state one measurable learning objective.
  2. Choose a local transportation problem and activate prior knowledge with a prediction.
  3. Model the investigation and specify what students record or produce.
  4. Ask learners to interpret the evidence, compare alternatives, and explain a decision.
  5. Add a low-cost pathway and at least two accessibility supports.
  6. Define an exit ticket or performance task that directly measures the objective.

Submission: A one- to two-page classroom adaptation plan and the evidence from your investigation. Submit through your course LMS or the method provided by the instructor; this public website does not transmit assignments.
