Weeks 9–10

Unit 5: Safety, Accessibility, and Equity

Audit and redesign a pedestrian crossing to support different needs.

Duration
Four hours across two weeks
Materials
Paper and pencil; browser optional
Evidence
Annotated crossing redesign, criteria/constraints matrix, and short rationale.
Unit sequenceObjectives1 Learn2 Experience3 Adapt4 Apply5 ReflectKnowledge check

Learning objectives

  1. Identify safety and accessibility features of pedestrian crossings.
  2. Evaluate a crossing using evidence from multiple users and contexts.
  3. Propose a feasible redesign with criteria, constraints, and trade-offs.
  4. Plan an inclusive classroom activity without requiring students to disclose disabilities.

Step 1 · Learn

Understand the concept

A crossing may offer a visual WALK symbol but still be difficult for people who cannot rely on vision. Other barriers include insufficient crossing time, uneven curb ramps, traffic speed, or missing accessible signals. Inclusive design asks whose needs have been overlooked.

Guiding question: Audit and redesign a pedestrian crossing to support different needs.

Estimated learning time: 35 minutes. Read the explanation, create a brief concept sketch, and identify one transportation example in your community.

Step 2 · Experience

Model investigation: Pedestrian crossings for different users

Allow 60–75 minutes. Work with a partner where possible. Record your reasoning, not just the final answer.

  1. Review a fictional crossing with a visual signal, no audible or tactile information, a short crossing interval, and an uneven curb.
  2. List the users who might experience barriers without assuming everyone has the same needs.
  3. Set criteria for safety, accessibility, cost, and maintenance.
  4. Sketch a revised crossing with at least two complementary signals (visual plus audible/tactile) and an accessible path.
  5. Explain how you would collect community input respectfully and without identifying students.
  6. Use a peer checklist to evaluate the design and make one revision.

Evidence to keep: Annotated crossing redesign, criteria/constraints matrix, and short rationale.

Technology and unplugged pathways

With technology: Use a shared document, spreadsheet, digital diagram, or classroom presentation to collect and explain evidence. Without technology: Use printed scenario cards, graph paper, drawings, and group discussion to demonstrate the same objective.

Accessibility: Provide written and spoken directions, readable diagrams with text labels, flexible response formats, and group roles. Do not rely only on color or on personal student disclosures.

Standards connection

MS-ETS1-1; MS-ETS1-2 (engineering design connections); verify Alabama subject standards locally. These are suggested connections; check the exact performance expectation and your assessment evidence before claiming full standards alignment.

Stage 6 · Teaching and assessment guide

Make the investigation classroom-ready

Teacher implementation sequence

  1. Introduce (5–10 minutes): Present a real community transportation situation and ask students to make an initial prediction.
  2. Define (10 minutes): State the challenge, criteria, constraints, and the evidence students will collect.
  3. Investigate (20–30 minutes): Complete the accessible crossing redesign in pairs; record observations in a labeled table or diagram.
  4. Analyze (15 minutes): Compare results, discuss alternative explanations, and identify limitations of the model.
  5. Improve (15 minutes): Revise a decision or design using evidence, and explain the expected benefit.
  6. Assess (5–10 minutes): Collect an exit ticket that includes a claim, supporting evidence, and one remaining question.

Evidence of learning

Evaluate two crossing designs against agreed safety, accessibility, and cost criteria using a scoring matrix.

Subject and grade adaptations

Grade 6 social studies: identify access barriers in different neighborhoods. Grade 7 ELA: argue for an inclusive design using evidence. Grade 8 engineering: score two alternatives and justify trade-offs.

Step 4 · Apply

Classroom transfer plan

  1. Identify the target grade and subject, and state one measurable learning objective.
  2. Choose a local transportation problem and activate prior knowledge with a prediction.
  3. Model the investigation and specify what students record or produce.
  4. Ask learners to interpret the evidence, compare alternatives, and explain a decision.
  5. Add a low-cost pathway and at least two accessibility supports.
  6. Define an exit ticket or performance task that directly measures the objective.

Submission: A one- to two-page classroom adaptation plan and the evidence from your investigation. Submit through your course LMS or the method provided by the instructor; this public website does not transmit assignments.
